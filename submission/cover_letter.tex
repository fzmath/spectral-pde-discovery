\opening{Dear Editor,}

We are pleased to submit our manuscript entitled \textbf{``Spatiotemporal spectral derivatives with forward stepwise BIC for robust partial differential equation discovery''} by Fuchang Wang and Huirong Cao for consideration as a regular article in \textit{Applied Mathematics and Computation}.

\textbf{Motivation.} Data-driven discovery of partial differential equations (PDEs) from noisy simulation or experimental data has attracted substantial recent interest. However, two persistent barriers limit practical deployment: (i) computing high-order spatial derivatives (e.g., $u_{xxxx}$) from noisy data amplifies noise catastrophically, and (ii) standard sparse regression solvers such as STLSQ require manual threshold tuning that is problem-dependent and noise-sensitive. Existing spectral methods, while spectrally accurate, assume periodic boundaries and fail on non-periodic solutions due to Gibbs ringing.

\textbf{Our contributions.} We propose a framework that addresses both barriers through three interconnected components:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Spatiotemporal spectral derivatives (algorithm innovation).} Instead of applying the Fourier transform slice-by-slice in space, we compute a single two-dimensional transform over the joint space-time domain. This exploits the traveling-wave structure of PDE solutions: the spectral energy of a wave $u(x,t)=f(x-ct)$ concentrates along the characteristic line $k_t=-ck_x$, while boundary-induced Gibbs noise spreads isotropically. A 2D low-pass filter then separates signal from noise. We prove (Proposition~2) that the spectrum of a traveling wave lies on a one-dimensional manifold, making this separation mathematically principled rather than empirical.

\item \textbf{Spectral weak formulation (robustness innovation).} Rather than computing derivatives pointwise and then regressing, we formulate the discovery problem in weak form: both sides of the PDE are integrated against localized Gaussian test functions before regression. This integration acts as an additional low-pass filter specifically for high-order derivatives. We prove (Proposition~1) that the spatiotemporal spectral derivatives achieve spectral convergence with an explicit error bound involving both truncation and filtering terms.

\item \textbf{Normalized forward stepwise BIC (automation innovation).} Each candidate library term is normalized to unit $\ell_2$ norm before regression, eliminating scale bias. Forward stepwise selection with the Bayesian information criterion then determines sparsity automatically---no threshold grid search is required. We prove (Proposition~3) that the BIC criterion selects the true support with probability tending to one under standard restricted eigenvalue and minimum coefficient conditions.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Experimental validation.} We benchmark on the MDBench standard suite, covering four 1D PDEs (Burgers, KdV, Kuramoto--Sivashinsky, non-periodic advection) at five noise levels, plus three higher-dimensional problems (2D advection-diffusion, 2D reaction-diffusion, 2D/3D heat conduction). We compare against three baselines: SR3 with finite differences, \emph{spatial-only} spectral derivatives with exponential filtering (the closest prior method, Schaeffer and McCalla, 2017), and Weak SINDy. The control-variable comparison against spatial-only spectral methods is particularly informative: both use identical exponential filtering and BIC selection, yet the spatiotemporal version recovers the non-periodic advection equation exactly at clean data while the spatial version fails completely (0/1 true terms). On periodic problems, our method achieves comparable or superior accuracy to Weak SINDy at 10--20$\times$ lower computational cost.

\textbf{Fit with AMC.} We believe this manuscript is well-suited to \textit{Applied Mathematics and Computation} because it combines: (i) a genuinely new algorithmic idea (spatiotemporal spectral differentiation for PDE discovery), (ii) rigorous mathematical analysis (three propositions with complete proofs), and (iii) extensive computational validation on a public benchmark against established baselines. The method requires no GPU and runs on a standard CPU, making it reproducible and accessible.
